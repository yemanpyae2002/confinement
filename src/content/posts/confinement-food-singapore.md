**Short answer: confinement food is the warming, protein-and-iron-rich diet eaten for about a month after birth — sesame oil chicken, fish and papaya soup, pork trotter in vinegar, red date tea. In Singapore, 28 days of daily delivery costs roughly S$800–S$1,500.** Most of the "avoid" list is cultural rather than medical, but the core diet is genuinely well-suited to postpartum recovery.

## Why this particular menu

Strip away the tradition and the logic holds up. After birth you've lost blood, you're depleted of iron, and if you're breastfeeding you need roughly an extra 500 calories a day plus a lot of fluid. Now look at what confinement cuisine actually consists of:

- **Fish, chicken, pork liver, eggs** — high-quality protein for tissue repair
- **Pork liver, red dates, black fungus** — iron, for replacing what you lost
- **Papaya, green papaya soup, fish soup** — traditionally galactagogues, believed to support milk supply
- **Ginger, sesame oil, rice wine** — warming and, in TCM terms, promoting circulation
- **Soups, red date tea, longan tea** — hydration, which matters enormously when breastfeeding

The tradition arrived at a sensible postpartum diet centuries before anyone was measuring micronutrients. That's the part worth keeping.

## The classic dishes

| Dish | What it is | Why it's eaten |
|---|---|---|
| Sesame oil chicken (麻油鸡) | Chicken in sesame oil, ginger and rice wine | The signature confinement dish — warming, protein-rich |
| Fish and papaya soup | Green papaya simmered with fish | Traditionally believed to support milk supply |
| Pork trotter in black vinegar | Trotters slow-cooked in sweet black vinegar with ginger | Collagen and calcium; usually from week two |
| Red date tea (红枣茶) | Red dates, longan, sometimes ginger | Iron, and the everyday confinement drink |
| Ginger vinegar egg | Eggs in vinegar and ginger | Protein and iron, cheap and easy |
| Pork liver soup | Liver in ginger broth | Very high in iron |
| Fish maw soup | Fish maw with herbs | Collagen; a "special occasion" dish |

**Malay confinement food** centres on warming spices and herbs — turmeric, ginger, lemongrass — with dishes like *ayam masak halia* (ginger chicken) and herbal drinks such as *jamu*. **Indian postpartum food** uses dry ginger, fenugreek, ajwain and garlic, often in *kanji* (rice porridge) and ghee-rich preparations, all intended to warm the body and support milk supply.

Several Singapore providers cook halal confinement menus and Indian-style postpartum food — filter by what you need rather than assuming everything is Chinese-style.

## What to actually avoid

Here's where tradition and medicine diverge. Being straight about it:

**Genuinely worth limiting:**
- **Alcohol** — including the rice wine in confinement dishes if you're breastfeeding. Most of the alcohol cooks off in long-simmered dishes, but ask providers to reduce or omit it if you're concerned
- **High caffeine** — up to about 200mg a day (roughly two cups of coffee) is generally considered fine while breastfeeding
- **High-mercury fish** — king mackerel, swordfish, shark. Standard advice, not confinement-specific
- **Raw and undercooked foods** — normal food safety, particularly when your immune system is recovering

**Traditionally avoided, but no clinical basis:**
- **Cold and iced drinks** — no evidence they harm recovery. If a cold drink is what you want in Singapore's heat, have one
- **"Cooling" foods** — cucumber, watermelon, cabbage. These are TCM classifications, not nutritional ones
- **Eggs** — occasionally discouraged in some family traditions, but eggs are actively *encouraged* in mainstream Chinese confinement practice. Eat them
- **Milo** — safe. Small caffeine content, far below coffee
- **Salt** — some traditions restrict it heavily. Very low-sodium diets aren't necessary and make food unpleasant enough that people stop eating properly

The practical risk in over-restricting is real: if the food is joyless, you eat less, and undereating during recovery and breastfeeding is a genuine problem. A diet you'll actually finish beats a theoretically perfect one you won't.

**One important exception:** if you had gestational diabetes, the classic confinement menu is heavy on rice wine, sweet vinegar and longan — all high in sugar. Tell your provider. Most kitchens will adjust, and it's worth checking with your doctor about post-delivery glucose monitoring.

## Choosing a provider

Things that actually matter, in order:

1. **Do a trial day first.** You're committing to 56 meals. Taste them. This is the single most useful thing you can do.
2. **Delivery window.** Some deliver both meals in one morning drop (you reheat dinner), others deliver twice. If you dislike reheated food, check.
3. **Menu rotation.** Ask how many days before dishes repeat. By week three, a short rotation gets grim.
4. **Dietary flexibility.** Gestational diabetes, no alcohol, halal, vegetarian, no sesame oil — ask at enquiry, not after you've paid.
5. **Start-date flexibility.** Babies don't arrive on schedule. Confirm you can shift your start date without penalty — this catches people out constantly.
6. **Packaging.** Some use insulated tiffin carriers you return; others disposable containers. Matters if nobody's home at delivery time.

## If you're cooking it yourself

Entirely doable, especially with a partner or parent helping. Three things make it easier:

- **Batch cook and freeze before you deliver.** Sesame oil chicken, soups and red date tea all freeze well. A weekend of cooking in month eight buys you two weeks of meals.
- **Buy a slow cooker.** Most of these dishes are long-simmer, low-attention. A slow cooker turns them into a 10-minute task.
- **Brew red date tea in bulk.** A large pot lasts two to three days refrigerated, and it's the thing you'll drink most.

Realistically, most families combine: delivery for the first two weeks when you're least mobile, then home cooking once you're moving again.

## The bottom line

The core confinement diet is genuinely good postpartum nutrition — protein, iron, fluids, warm and easy to eat one-handed. Keep that. Drop the restrictions that just make eating miserable, and tell your provider about any medical conditions rather than quietly suffering through a menu that doesn't suit you.
