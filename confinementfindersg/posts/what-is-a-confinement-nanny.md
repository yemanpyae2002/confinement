**Short answer: a confinement nanny (*pui yuet* in Cantonese, *yue sao* in Mandarin) is an experienced carer who lives in your home for around 28 days after birth, cooking confinement meals, handling night feeds and newborn care, and supporting your recovery. In Singapore she costs roughly S$3,000–S$4,500 for 28 days through an agency**, plus the customary red packet and, for a foreign nanny, a monthly government levy.

She is not a maid, not a nurse and not a babysitter. The role is specific: get the mother recovered and the newborn thriving, for one month.

## What a confinement nanny actually does

Duties vary between individuals, so confirm the list before you book. A standard stay-in placement usually covers:

**For the baby**
- Night feeds, nappy changes and settling — she typically sleeps near the baby
- Bathing, including the traditional herbal bath if you want it
- Baby laundry, sterilising bottles, and monitoring feeding and weight
- Umbilical cord care in the early days

**For you**
- Cooking confinement meals — usually three meals plus soups and red date tea daily
- Herbal baths, and in many cases a simple postnatal massage
- Breastfeeding support: helping with latching, expressing and supply
- Keeping visitors managed so you can sleep

**What she typically does *not* do**: general housework, cooking for the rest of the family, caring for older children, or heavy cleaning. Some nannies will help with light tidying, but a confinement nanny is not a domestic helper, and treating her as one is the fastest route to a failed placement.

## What it costs in Singapore

| Arrangement | Typical cost (28 days) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Stay-in, via agency | S$3,000 – S$4,500 | Most common. Includes agency placement fee |
| Stay-in, direct hire | S$2,600 – S$3,800 | Cheaper, but no vetting or replacement guarantee |
| Daytime only (~12 hrs) | S$2,200 – S$3,200 | Suits families with a partner on leave at night |
| Peak-period surcharge | +S$300 – S$800 | Chinese New Year and auspicious dates |

**Additional costs to plan for:**

- **Red packet (*ang bao*)**: customarily S$100–S$300, given at the end of the placement. Not optional in practice — it's an established part of the arrangement.
- **Her food**: she eats what she cooks, so factor in the extra groceries.
- **MOM levy** for a foreign nanny: **S$60 per month** if your newborn is a Singapore citizen, or **S$300 per month** if not.
- **Medical insurance**: employers are required to buy medical insurance covering the nanny — the minimum coverage requirement was raised to S$60,000 per year for policies starting on or after 1 July 2023. Confirm the current figure with MOM or your agency at the time of hiring.
- **Replacement or extension**: ask what a replacement costs if the placement doesn't work, and what the daily rate is beyond 28 days.

## The work permit rules, briefly

Most confinement nannies in Singapore are Malaysian, and they require a Work Permit for confinement nanny from the Ministry of Manpower. The key conditions:

- The permit is valid for a **maximum of 16 weeks from your child's date of birth** — apply late and you get a shorter window
- **You must be at least 21** at the time of application, with a **newborn under 4 months old**
- The nanny must be **Malaysian, aged 23 to 70**, and must work at your residence
- A medical examination is optional, but MOM recommends one given she'll be handling your newborn

Agencies normally file this for you, but **you are the legal employer of record**, not the agency. Get in writing who is applying, what the levy will be, and what happens if the permit is rejected. Check the current rules directly on the [MOM confinement nanny work permit page](https://www.mom.gov.sg/passes-and-permits/work-permit-for-confinement-nanny/eligibility) — levy amounts and insurance minimums change.

## Agency or direct hire?

**Go through an agency if** this is your first baby, you want a replacement guarantee, or you'd rather not handle the work permit paperwork yourself. You're paying a few hundred dollars for vetting and a fallback. Browse [confinement nanny agencies in Singapore](/nanny-agencies/) to compare.

**Direct hire works if** you have a personal recommendation from someone who has actually used her, and you're comfortable managing the permit application and having no backup if she falls through mid-placement. It's cheaper, and the risk is real but manageable when the referral is solid.

Either way, book early. Experienced nannies with good referrals are commonly reserved four to six months ahead, and Chinese New Year is the hardest period to staff.

## Questions worth asking before you commit

1. **How many years, and how many babies?** Experience with newborns specifically, not just childcare.
2. **Which dialects and languages?** If she can't communicate with you directly, day-to-day friction adds up fast.
3. **Where will she sleep?** Most expect a bed in the baby's room or a spare room. Clarify what you can offer before booking.
4. **What's her cooking like — and can she adapt?** If you have gestational diabetes, are vegetarian, or can't stomach heavy sesame oil, ask now.
5. **Breastfeeding stance.** Some nannies push formula to get the baby sleeping through. If you're committed to breastfeeding, say so explicitly and confirm she'll support it.
6. **Day off arrangements.** Most work six days a week. Find out which day and what your cover plan is.
7. **What happens if it isn't working?** Ask the agency directly about the replacement process and timeline.

That last one matters more than people expect. A nanny living in your home for a month during your most vulnerable weeks is an intense arrangement, and personality fit is not a luxury consideration.

## Nanny or confinement centre?

The honest trade-off: a **nanny** gives you one-to-one care in your own home for roughly a third of the price of a centre, but you need a spare room and you're managing an employment relationship while recovering. A **[confinement centre](/confinement-centres/)** hands you a fully serviced environment with no management overhead, but costs S$9,000–S$16,000 for 28 days and means moving out of your home with a newborn.
