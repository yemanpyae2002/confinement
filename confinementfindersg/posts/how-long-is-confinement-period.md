**Short answer: 28 to 30 days in Chinese tradition, 44 days in Malay tradition, and about 40 days in Indian tradition.** Medically, postpartum recovery takes roughly six weeks regardless of culture. In Singapore, the 28-day figure dominates commercially — nearly every confinement centre package, nanny placement and meal plan is sold in 28-day blocks.

## The numbers at a glance

| Tradition | Length | Local name | Typical focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chinese | 28–30 days | 坐月子 (*zuo yue zi*) | Warmth, avoiding "wind", ginger and sesame oil dishes, herbal soups |
| Malay | 44 days | *Berpantang* | Jamu massage, *bengkung* binding, herbal baths, warming diet |
| Indian | ~40 days | *Jaappa* / *Sooth* | Oil massage, warming spices, rest with female relatives |
| Medical | ~6 weeks | Postpartum period | Uterine involution, wound healing, the six-week check |

The traditions land close to the same place, which is not a coincidence — they all track the same underlying biology.

## Why 28 days in Chinese tradition

The Chinese month is lunar, and *zuo yue zi* literally means "sitting the month". A lunar cycle is roughly 29.5 days, so 28 to 30 days is simply one moon. Some families extend to 40 days after a caesarean or a difficult delivery, and some count 30 days for a boy and 28 for a girl, though that varies by dialect group and family.

Practically, in Singapore this is the number that shapes the market. When a centre quotes you a package or an agency quotes a nanny, assume 28 days unless stated otherwise, and check the daily rate for extending.

## Why 44 days in Malay tradition

Malay *berpantang* is the longest of the three at 44 days, and it is the most structured around physical treatment rather than just rest. The period typically includes a course of jamu massage, daily *bengkung* binding of the abdomen, herbal baths and *tungku* (a warm stone compress on the belly).

The extra two weeks aren't arbitrary — the tradition treats the full closing of the body as a gradual process, and the massage and binding schedule is spread across the entire window rather than front-loaded.

## Why about 40 days in Indian tradition

Indian postpartum practice runs around 40 days, often spent at the mother's family home, with daily oil massage for both mother and baby and a diet built on warming spices such as dry ginger, fenugreek and ajwain. The number 40 recurs across South Asian, Middle Eastern and Latin American postpartum traditions — the *cuarentena* in Spanish is literally "the forty".

## What medicine says

The clinical postpartum period is about six weeks, which is when most women have their postnatal check. In that window:

- The **uterus** contracts from roughly 1kg back to around 60g, a process called involution, largely complete by six weeks.
- **Lochia** — postpartum bleeding — typically lasts two to six weeks.
- **Perineal tears or an episiotomy** generally heal within two to three weeks.
- A **caesarean wound** takes about six weeks to heal properly, with heavy lifting discouraged throughout.

So the traditional 28 to 44 day windows sit sensibly inside a six-week recovery. Where they undershoot is the tail end: at day 29 you are not fully recovered just because the package ended.

## How long should *you* book?

Be realistic rather than traditional. A few patterns that hold up in practice:

**Book the full 28 days if** it's your first baby, you had a caesarean, you're recovering from a complicated delivery, you're breastfeeding and finding it hard, or you have no family support nearby.

**14 to 21 days can be enough if** it's a second or third baby, you had a straightforward vaginal delivery, and you have a partner on leave or family close by. Many Singapore mums do exactly this for baby number two.

**Consider extending past 28 days if** you had a difficult caesarean recovery, you're dealing with a tongue-tie or feeding problems, or you have twins — in which case most people who've done it will tell you to book longer than you think.

The first fortnight is the part that matters most. That's when you are least mobile, most sleep-deprived and most likely to be establishing feeding. If budget forces a choice, spend it on the front half.

## What it costs by length

Most providers price per day beyond the standard block, so shortening or extending is usually straightforward.

- **Confinement centre**: roughly S$9,000–S$16,000 for 28 days at a standard centre. Shorter stays are pro-rated, often at a slightly higher daily rate.
- **Confinement nanny**: about S$3,000–S$4,500 for 28 days stay-in through an agency. Note that a foreign nanny's work permit is capped at 16 weeks from the child's date of birth, so a long extension has an outer limit.
- **Meal delivery**: around S$800–S$1,500 for 28 days, with 7 and 14-day plans widely available — the easiest option to extend if you decide you want more.

## Booking timing

Whatever length you choose, book earlier than feels necessary. Good [confinement centres](/confinement-centres/) and experienced nannies are commonly reserved four to six months ahead in Singapore, and periods around auspicious dates fill first. Enquiring costs nothing and most providers will hold a slot with a small deposit.
